Abstract—Transmitting multimedia files such as audio is a challenging task in secure wireless communication systems. Therefore, encryption methods are required to secure audio content. This work suggests an audio encryption method that gives an effective security level. To achieve this, the secret key is extracted from the wireless channel characteristics and then processed through Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) in Counter (CTR) mode to generate the corresponding subkeys for the audio data blocks. Each subkey is divided into two parts, where the first part is XORed with the information bits containing the audio data, while the remaining part is assigned to the frozen bits. The simulation results demonstrate that the proposed method performs well and is effective against various types of attacks. 
Keywords—Audio encryption, Polar codes, Information bits, Frozen bits, Secret key, Wireless channel characteristics.
Introduction 
The security of audio signal plays an important role in 5G networks, as audio signals not only facilitate convenient information transmission and communication but also create potential opportunities for criminal misuse [1]. The encryption methods are employed to protect the audio signals. However, audio signal is more difficult to encrypt than other types of data since there is the association between adjacent samples. Various audio encryption methods have been proposed, such as mathematical-based approaches [2], [3], chaos-based schemes [4]–[9], or chaotic maps combined with DNA [10], [11]. Mathematical-based audio encryption methods typically exhibit weak security due to their deterministic structure, limited randomness, and vulnerability to statistical and cryptanalytic attacks. Chaos-based audio encryption schemes provide high sensitivity and randomness. However, they often suffer from parameter instability and limited cryptographic rigor. Many of these methods are vulnerable to known-plaintext and differential attacks if not carefully designed. Moreover, their computational complexity can be relatively high. Although DNA-based approaches introduce additional randomness, they often incur high complexity and lack strong theoretical security guarantees. In addition, their practical implementation is relatively challenging.
In recent years, crypto-coding technique has emerged as one of the physical-layer security approaches that has attracted considerable research attention, as it enables joint data encryption and channel coding to be performed in a single step. Crypto-coding technique can be implemented on Turbo codes [12]–[15] and polar codes [16]–[19]. However, crypto-coding techniques based on polar codes offer more significant advantages, as polar codes are currently adopted in fifth generation (5G) networks. These crypto-coding based polar codes use a secret key generated from the characteristics of the wireless channel to manipulate the information bits and frozen bits. The security effectiveness of these methods is only validated through evaluating their resistance to passive eavesdropping attacks, while neglecting their capability to encrypt different types of data and their robustness against other active attacks. Moreover, using a single secret key generated from the characteristics of the wireless channel to encrypt all data blocks may compromise security if the system is subjected to active attacks.
This study proposes an audio encryption method based on crypto-coding with polar codes. The proposed method is implemented by using a secret key generated from the characteristics of the wireless channel to derive subkeys corresponding to the input audio blocks through the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) key derivation function in Counter (CTR) mode. Each subkey is divided into two parts corresponding to the information bits and the frozen bits. The first part of the subkey is XORed with the audio data embedded in the information bits, while the second part is assigned to the frozen bits. This research is developed from our study in [19]. The simulation results show that the proposed method encrypts/decrypts well and is highly effective in resisting attacks on encrypted audio files. Several contributions are summarized as follows:
The AES key derivation function in CTR mode is incorporated to enhance the security of the secret key generated from the characteristics of the wireless channel.
The audio data contained in the information bits are protected using one part of the subkey, while the remaining part is stored in the frozen bits to enhance diffusion and confusion. This method not only improves audio encryption performance but also resists passive eavesdropping attacks and ensures the reliability of the audio data transmitted over wireless channels.

 Spectrogram Analysis: Fig. 2 compares the energy spectrograms of the original and encrypted audio signals.  Comparison with Existing Methods: Tables V and VI present a comprehensive performance comparison between the proposed method and the related works reported in [2], [10], [13], [15], and [16]. In terms of signal structure disruption, the proposed scheme achieves correlation coefficients that approach the ideal value of zero, indicating effective decorrelation. The obtained results are comparable to, and in some cases superior to, those of existing methods. Specifically, for the “Human” audio sample, the correlation coefficient achieved by the proposed method is 0.00034, which is lower than the value of −0.0005 reported in [13]. Similarly, for the Animal sample, the proposed method attains a correlation coefficient of −0.00098, demonstrating stronger statistical independence compared with the value of 0.0014 in [13] and significantly outperforming the result of −0.0274 reported in [16].
The most significant advantage of the proposed method lies in its information security metrics. In terms of entropy, the experimental results consistently reach values of approximately 7.9989, which are extremely close to the ideal value of 8.0. These results are significantly higher than those reported in [2] (ranging from 7.9841 to 7.9862) and [10] (7.9902 – 7.9915), indicating that the generated ciphertext exhibits a higher degree of randomness and chaos. Regarding resistance to differential attacks, the proposed scheme also demonstrates superior performance. The average NPCR value reaches approximately 99.61%, which is closer to the theoretical value compared with the range of 99.56%–99.59% reported in [2], the UACI values also remain stable around the ideal level of 33.46%. This close proximity to the theoretical limits confirms the extremely high sensitivity of the proposed algorithm (i.e., a strong butterfly effect), ensuring robust security against modern cryptanalysis techniques. Notably, the proposed method shows a clear advantage in encryption quality as measured by PSNR. While the methods in [15] and [16] report positive PSNR values (4.5299 dB and 4.373 dB, respectively), and the method in [13] achieves PSNR values ranging from −8.50 dB to −14.47 dB, the proposed scheme consistently attains much lower values, ranging from −29.24 dB to −29.45 dB. This substantial difference confirms that the proposed ciphertext contains significantly higher noise energy, thereby providing more effective concealment of the original signal compared with the reference methods.

   Chosen/Known Plaintext Analysis: Known Plaintext Attacks (KPA) and Chosen-Plaintext Attacks (CPA) typically rely on discovering linear relationships or deterministic patterns between the plaintext and the ciphertext in order to infer the secret key. The proposed method effectively resists these types of attacks, as demonstrated through both theoretical analysis and experimental evaluation. Theoretically: The system employs AES in CTR mode to generate the keystream. Owing to the nonlinear nature of AES and the use of a varying Initialization Vector (IV), each data block is encrypted with a unique subkey. Consequently, deriving the original secret key from plaintext–ciphertext pairs is computationally impossible. Experimentally: We conducted simulations of two hypothetical attack scenarios to verify the security of the proposed scheme:
    KPA scenario: It is assumed that the attacker knows the plaintext of the “Animal” audio file and extracts the corresponding keystream (Key A). The attacker then reuses this keystream to decrypt another file, “Drum” (encrypted with Key B). The results show that the two keystreams are completely different. The decryption error map in Fig. 6 indicates a Bit Error Rate (BER) of 49.56%, meaning the decrypted output is essentially random noise.
    CPA scenario: The attacker selects a special input, namely an all-zero sequence (“Silence”), feeds it into the system, and obtains the corresponding keystream (Key C). This keystream is then used to decrypt the “Drum” file. The results (Fig. 7) show a BER of 50.08%. BER values close to 50% in both scenarios confirm that the system achieves strong confusion and diffusion properties. Even a minor change in the plaintext or the key-generation parameters (IV/Counter) leads to a completely different keystream, effectively preventing any reuse of information from KPA/CPA attacks.
